# Shared patterns

## Pattern

A **pattern** is a described piece of business that recurs from one project to the next: memberships with recurring dues, events with registration and pricing, donations, volunteer scheduling, a library of contents with access rules, following up the people an organisation helps.

Each of these has been specified from scratch, by someone, hundreds of times.

A pattern holds:

- the **domain description** — the concepts, their rules, their vocabulary, written exactly as a [specification](specification.md) is;
- the **[stories](stories.md)** that usually come with it;
- **fictional data** covering the ordinary cases and the awkward ones;
- the **questions it always raises** — the decisions every project using it has had to make. This is often the most valuable part: it turns a maker's experience into a checklist that a first-time maker also gets.

A pattern does **not** hold a [brand](brand.md) or a screen design. Those belong to the project.

## Adopting a pattern

A project **adopts** a pattern by copying it into its own specification and adapting it: renaming concepts to the customer's words, deleting what does not apply, adding what is missing.

**Rule.** The copy belongs to the project. It does not stay linked to the pattern, and the pattern changing later never changes a project that adopted it.

This is deliberate. A pattern is a good starting point that a project immediately owns and is free to diverge from. Something shared that kept changing underneath a project would be something the project relies on and does not control — exactly what [handover](delivery.md) forbids.

## Contributing a pattern

A project that has described a piece of business well can offer it back, generalised: the customer's specific words removed, the questions it raised written down.

Contribution needs the **customer's consent**, and nothing of the customer's own business — their data, their particular rules, their identity — leaves with it.

## Why they are held in common

The tenth association needing memberships should not pay to describe memberships for the tenth time. Nor should the hundredth.

Patterns are the part of Supersoft that compounds. Every project makes the next one cheaper, and the projects that benefit most are the ones with the least money to spend — which is the point.

**Rule.** Patterns are common property. They are not a paid tier, not a differentiator, and not something a maker can enclose.
